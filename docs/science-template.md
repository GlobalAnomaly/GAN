# Science Entry Template & House Style

*The rulebook for the "life elsewhere" section — exoplanets, the search for life, technosignatures, interstellar objects, and related discoveries. A parallel content type to UFO cases: same infrastructure, different template. Its two jobs: make real science genuinely understandable to non-scientists, and never overhype it.*

---

## 1. How this differs from a UFO case

A UFO case is a *reported phenomenon* relayed neutrally — a video, a location, a confirmed/unverified/debunked label. A science entry is a *published or official finding* explained plainly — a paper or agency announcement, an institution, an image. They share the admin panel, the review inbox, and the home page, but almost nothing else.

Keep the two visibly distinct. A sighting never carries the standing of a peer-reviewed result, and the site should never let the reader confuse the two. That separation isn't a limitation — it's what lets the science section lend credibility to the whole site instead of blurring into it.

**No confirmed/unverified/debunked labels here.** Science entries aren't sightings to be validated; they're findings to be explained. Their "status" is the finding's own maturity: *candidate*, *proposed*, *confirmed*, *disputed*, *superseded* — the language the scientists themselves use.

---

## 2. The house voice

Written for a curious non-scientist, not for a journal. The reader is smart but hasn't studied astrophysics, so the writing does the translation work for them — without dumbing down to the point of being wrong.

Two forces held in balance:
- **Clarity** — explain everything; assume no prior knowledge.
- **Accuracy** — never trade a true-but-complex statement for a false-but-simple one. A candidate biosignature is not "aliens found." Simplify the *language*, never the *facts*.

The voice is still neutral relay: report what the study found and what its authors claim, attributed to them. You're the translator, not the cheerleader and not the skeptic.

---

## 3. The plain-language discipline

Concrete rules that make science readable:

**Lead with meaning, then mechanism.** Open with what the finding *means* to a human before how it was found. "Astronomers found a planet where it might rain iron" first; the spectroscopy second.

**Define every technical term in a few words, on first use.** Not "they detected a biosignature" but "they detected a biosignature, a gas that on Earth is only produced by living things." One clause, no jargon left standing.

**One good analogy per entry.** Analogies are how non-experts actually grasp scale and process. "The star dims slightly each time the planet crosses in front of it, like a moth passing a streetlight." Use one strong one; don't pile them up.

**Short sentences. Concrete images. Human scale.** "1,400 light-years away" means little; "so far that the light we see left before the fall of Rome" lands.

**Always answer "why does this matter?"** Every entry earns its place by telling the reader why they should care: a step toward finding life, a record broken, an assumption overturned.

**Write like a human, not a model.** Same rule as the UFO section, and it matters even more here where the temptation to sound "science-y" is strong. No em dashes, ever (use commas, colons, parentheses, or separate sentences). Vary sentence rhythm, cut stock phrasing ("genuinely," "testament to," "delve," "it's not just X, it's Y"), and don't tie every paragraph up with a bow. Plain and slightly uneven beats polished.

## 4. The anti-hype rule (the credibility keystone)

This is the science section's version of the attribution rule, and it's non-negotiable, because space-and-aliens content is where sensationalism is most tempting and most damaging.

- **Attribute the claim to the study or institution.** "A NASA team reports," "the paper suggests," "ESA announced" — never the site asserting a discovery in its own voice.
- **Respect the finding's actual maturity.** "Candidate," "possible," "consistent with," and "suggests" are not decoration — they're the difference between honest and misleading. A gas *consistent with* life is not evidence *of* life.
- **Every entry has a "what this does not mean" slot.** The single most important habit here. When a study finds a promising signal, state plainly what it doesn't establish — that a biosignature candidate isn't confirmed life, that an interstellar object being unusual doesn't make it a spacecraft. This is what earns a skeptical reader's trust, and it's what separates you from every breathless headline.

If an entry could be mistaken for a bigger claim than the scientists actually made, it's wrong — fix it before it publishes.

## 5. Fields of a science entry

- **Headline** — plain, accurate, no hype. "Webb telescope finds water vapor on a distant world," not "Life found on alien planet!"
- **Topic** — one of: exoplanets · search for life (SETI/technosignatures) · astrobiology/biosignatures · interstellar objects · space signals · missions & telescopes · other
- **Status** — candidate · proposed · confirmed · disputed · superseded
- **Institution(s) / source** — NASA, ESA, SETI Institute, a named university, a journal
- **Date**
- **Images** — telescope imagery, mission photos, diagrams, artist's impressions — each with its credit line (see Section 8)
- **Body** (structure in Section 6)
- **Sources** — the paper (with DOI/link), the press release, attributed

---

## 6. Structure of an entry

1. **What they found** — in plain terms, meaning first.
2. **How they found it** — briefly and accessibly; enough that the reader trusts it, not a methods section.
3. **Why it matters** — the stakes, in human terms.
4. **What this does not mean** — the honest limits; open questions.
5. **Sources** — paper and announcement, attributed.

---

## 7. Institutions & sources (worldwide) → this section's Tier A

Not just the American ones. A global net signals seriousness and broadens the content.

**Space agencies:** NASA (its Astrobiology Program, the NASA Exoplanet Archive run by NExScI at Caltech, JPL, and missions including JWST, TESS, Hubble, the Kepler legacy, and the upcoming Nancy Grace Roman telescope) · the European Space Agency (ESA) and its exoplanet missions · Japan's JAXA.

**Search-for-life & astrobiology:** the SETI Institute (technosignature searches, the Allen Telescope Array, LaserSETI, and exoplanet data work) · Breakthrough Listen (Berkeley) · university programs including Harvard's Galileo Project.

**Ground observatories:** the European Southern Observatory (ESO — the Very Large Telescope and the Extremely Large Telescope under construction in Chile) · the Vera C. Rubin Observatory · Green Bank · the Keck Observatory.

**Source-of-record journals:** Nature, Science, The Astrophysical Journal, and preprints on arXiv — useful for spotting findings early, but a preprint is not yet peer-reviewed, so flag that in "status."

**Access & image rule:** NASA imagery is generally free to use with a credit line (and not in a way implying endorsement); ESA and ESO release most imagery under liberal licenses — but verify per image and always credit. For journal papers, the *facts* are free to state and you link to the DOI, but don't reproduce copyrighted figures without checking the license. Press releases are meant to be shared; you still write your own plain-language account.

## 8. Worked example

**Topic:** interstellar objects **Status:** confirmed object, ongoing study
**Institution:** multiple, incl. SETI Institute; study in *Nature*
**Image:** telescope image of the object *(credit: observatory)*

**Headline:** A visitor from another star system is passing through — and it may be older than the Sun

**What they found:** Astronomers are tracking an object called 3I/ATLAS that didn't come from our solar system — it fell in from interstellar space, only the third such visitor we've ever caught. A study in the journal *Nature* suggests it could be extraordinarily old, perhaps around 12 billion years — nearly three times the age of the Sun.

**How they found it:** Its path and speed give it away: it's moving too fast and on the wrong kind of orbit to have formed here, which means it originated around another star and is just briefly crossing our neighborhood.

**Why it matters:** Interstellar objects are physical samples from other star systems arriving at our doorstep — a rare chance to study material that formed around a different sun without leaving home. Each one teaches us how common the ingredients of planets, and maybe life, are elsewhere.

**What this does not mean:** An unusual interstellar object is not evidence of alien technology. Its behavior so far is consistent with a natural body; the age estimate is an inference from its origin, not a certainty, and remains under study.

**Sources:** the *Nature* study (link); observatory announcement (link).

Notice: meaning first, one clear analogy-free hook, every claim attributed, and a "what this does not mean" that heads off the alien-spacecraft leap before a reader makes it.

---

## 9. Reviewer's checklist

Before a science entry publishes:
- Would a smart non-scientist understand every sentence? Is any jargon left undefined?
- Is the finding's maturity stated honestly (candidate vs confirmed)?
- Is every claim attributed to the study or institution, not asserted by the site?
- Is the "what this does not mean" section filled in and honest?
- Does the headline promise no more than the science delivers?
- Are all images credited and cleared for use?
