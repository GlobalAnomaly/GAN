# Editorial Template & House Style

*The rulebook every case account is written to — by the bot, by you, and by any moderator. Its job: make ten thousand entries feel like one coherent, trustworthy archive.*

---

## 1. Who uses this

Three audiences, one document:
- **The bot** — Section 8 is its instruction set; feed it verbatim.
- **You and your moderators** — Sections 2–7 are the style guide; Section 10 is the pre-publish checklist.
- **The reader** — never sees this, but feels it in every consistent, grounded entry.

---

## 2. The house voice

Serious content for genuine seekers — people who want real answers and haven't stopped wondering. Not a debunking skeptic site, not a breathless believer blog: a neutral archive that hands the reader credible information, keeps the genuine mystery alive, and lets them form their own view.

The voice is **reportage with curiosity**: relay what happened and who said it, and where something is truly unexplained, say so with the openness it deserves. No "astonishingly," no "the truth is finally out" — but equally, no "obviously just a drone," no smug dismissal. If a sentence tells the reader what to conclude in *either* direction, it's wrong.

**Neutral cuts both ways.** Leaning skeptic is exactly as much a bias as leaning believer — and for this audience it's the more damaging one, because dismissiveness reads as condescension to readers who came here precisely because no one has given them honest answers. The most neutral, most accurate, and most open-minded thing you can write about a genuinely unexplained case is simply: *no one has explained this — not the witnesses, not the investigators, not the government.* That isn't a weakness. It's the mystery, stated honestly, and it's what keeps the story alive. Admitting "this may be beyond what humans can currently explain" is not bad logic; it's just true.

**Wonder is earned, and it scales with credibility.** When a case is strong — named military aviators, radar and instrument data, official acknowledgment — lean into how genuinely remarkable it is; the credibility has earned the awe, and holding back would be its own distortion. When a case is thin, stay gentle and let the reader weigh it rather than manufacturing wonder it hasn't earned. Calibrate the openness to the evidence — never fake it, never withhold it when it's deserved.

**Invite the reader in.** You're asking the same questions they are, so say so. Ending an unexplained case with an honest "What do you think?" — or a reader poll, or open comments when they're on — tells the reader you don't have the answer either and you respect theirs. That shared not-knowing is the whole relationship: not an authority breaking their dreams with shaky logic, but a fellow seeker who takes the question seriously.

**The one line that still holds: open questions, yes; invented answers, no.** "What could possibly do this? No one knows" is open-minded and honest. "It was an alien craft" states as fact something nobody can. Wonder as boldly as the evidence allows, but never fill the mystery with a made-up answer. The certainty is what kills the story, in either direction; the open question is what keeps it alive.

**Write like a human, not a model.** Over-polished prose is its own credibility problem now. This audience spots machine-written text fast and trusts it less, so the writing has to read like a person wrote it:
- No em dashes. Ever. They are the single most recognized "this is AI" tell. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses, or just two sentences instead.
- Vary sentence length and rhythm. Drop the relentless three-part list and the too-neat parallel structure.
- Cut filler and stock phrasing: "genuinely," "truly," "delve," "testament to," "tapestry," "underscore," "boasts," and the "it's not just X, it's Y" and "that's the [X]" wrap-up constructions.
- Don't tie every paragraph up with a bow. A plain, slightly uneven voice reads as more honest than a buffed one.

## 3. The three core rules

**1. Attribution is load-bearing.** Every claim traces to a named or described source — "the pilot stated," "the report concludes," "witnesses said," "the uploader claims." The narration itself never asserts a UFO claim as fact. This single habit is what lets the site cover wild claims without becoming a wild-claims site.

**2. Credibility is shown, not asserted.** Never write "a highly credible witness." Write "a naval aviator with radar confirmation" and let the reader weigh it. Show the credentials, the instrument data, the corroboration — the credibility speaks for itself, and the classification label becomes something the account *demonstrates* rather than something stamped on top.

**3. Quote for information, not color.** Keep a quote only if removing it loses a fact, a specific, or a genuinely telling detail. "Accelerated beyond anything I'd trained against" stays — specific and informative. "It was terrifying" goes, unless the witness's fear is itself part of the documented record. Quotes are short and always attributed.

## 4. The grounding discipline

This is the rule that keeps synthesis from sliding into fiction. An account may contain only two kinds of statement:

- **What the evidence observably shows** — a bright object, holding position, drifting laterally. Not "a craft," not "metallic," not "disc-shaped," unless that is genuinely visible.
- **What people actually claim** — attributed clearly, never blended into the narration as fact.

And the load-bearing corollary: **unknowns stay unknown.** If the footage doesn't reveal the date, the shape, the altitude, or the location, the account *says so* rather than papering over the gap. The instinct — the bot's and the writer's — is to smooth a story by inventing the missing pieces. That instinct is the enemy. Every account has a dedicated slot for what's uncertain (Section 5) precisely so nothing gets smoothed away.

Never invent: object shape, material, size, altitude, time of day, number of witnesses, or location. If it isn't in the source, it isn't in the account. But read this rule correctly: stated openly, that uncertainty is not a debunk — it's the mystery preserved honestly. "What remains unknown" is where the wonder lives, not where it goes to die.

## 5. The fixed structure of every account

Every case page follows the same skeleton. Consistency is the point.

**Headline** — plain and descriptive, no hype. Names the observable and the location if known. *"Onlookers film silent object over Osaka skyline at dusk,"* not *"SHOCKING UFO caught on camera!"*

**Meta line** — classification · continent · country (or "Unknown") · date (or "Unknown") · source type.

**Body, in this exact order:**

1. **What the footage shows** — observable description only. The object, its behavior, the setting. No interpretation.
2. **What witnesses and officials say** — attributed testimony. Credentials shown. Load-bearing quotes only.
3. **Status and corroboration** — what any authority confirmed or denied; instrument or radar data; independent corroboration, or the lack of it.
4. **What remains unknown** — the explicit gaps. Location, date, object identity, whether it's been analyzed. This section is never empty for an unverified case; if you think it is, you've smoothed something over.

**Sources** — attributed links, one per source.

**Document link** — a "Read the full PDF report here" button pointing straight at the source, when one exists.

## 6. The classification rubric

Concrete criteria so the same evidence always gets the same label — whether the bot or a mod assigns it.

**Acknowledged** — a government or official body has publicly released or confirmed the material and offered no conventional explanation. The highest-credibility tier. *(Requires: official source.)*

**Unverified** — a public sighting with no official validation and no established conventional explanation. The default for most social and user-submitted content. Credibility *within* this tier still varies enormously by witness quality, instrument data, and corroboration — which is exactly why Rule 2 matters.

**Likely explained** — a plausible conventional cause is identified or strongly indicated (satellite train, drone, aircraft, balloon, lens flare, camera artifact, probable hoax) but not conclusively proven.

**Debunked** — a conventional cause is conclusively established, or the material is demonstrably fabricated. **Include these on purpose.** A directory that documents its debunks is far more trustworthy than one that only collects mysteries.

When in doubt between two labels, choose the more conservative one and state the uncertainty in "What remains unknown."

## 7. Copyright guardrails

Baked into the voice, not an afterthought:

- **Relay the witness, don't re-host the journalist.** A short, attributed quote from a pilot or official is exactly what fair use is built for. Lifting a paragraph from someone's *article about* that pilot is copying another writer's work — never do it.
- **The account is always original prose.** The bot may read another site to learn a case *exists* and grab the factual skeleton (date, place, who), then you write your own account. Other sites are leads, not content.
- **Facts aren't owned; expression is.** You can state the same facts; you cannot mirror another writer's wording, structure, or phrasing.
- **Link, don't copy, source documents.** The PDF button points at the original; your recap is in your words.

## 8. Bot instructions (paste verbatim)

> You are an editorial assistant for a neutral UFO/UAP archive. From the source material provided (video transcript, translation, article text, or PDF), write a single case account in the fixed structure below. You are a reporter relaying information, not an advocate.

> - Quote only short, load-bearing phrases that carry a fact or specific; attribute every quote. Cut anything kept for drama.
> - Write original prose. Do not copy or closely paraphrase the wording of any source article. State facts in your own words.
> - Show credibility through specifics (credentials, instrument data, corroboration); never assert it with adjectives.
> - Write in a plain, natural human voice. Do NOT use em dashes anywhere; use commas, colons, parentheses, or separate sentences. Vary sentence length. Avoid stock AI phrasing ("genuinely," "testament to," "tapestry," "delve," "it's not just X, it's Y," "that's the [X]"). The account must not read as machine-generated.

## 9. Worked example

**Raw input (literal translation of a clip):**
> [dusk, city rooftops] Voice 1: "What is that?" — Voice 2: "I don't know, it's not moving." — Voice 3: "It's going sideways now — look!" — [object leaves frame]

**Finished account:**

**Headline:** Onlookers film silent object over Osaka skyline at dusk

**Classification:** Unverified — a public clip with no official validation and no established conventional explanation.
**Location:** Asia · Japan  **Date:** Unknown  **Source:** YouTube (translated)

**What the footage shows:** A bright, compact object holds position above a city skyline at dusk. After several seconds it drifts laterally and leaves the frame. No sound is attributable to the object itself.

**What witnesses and officials say:** The people filming can be heard reacting with confusion, repeatedly asking one another what the object is. No individual is identified, and no official body is referenced.

**Status and corroboration:** No corroborating footage, instrument data, or official comment has been found. The uploader provides no further detail.

**What remains unknown:** The exact date and city are not confirmed by the source. The object's identity, size, and altitude cannot be determined from the footage. No analysis has been performed.

**Sources:** original upload (link).

Notice: the useless literal quotes ("What is that?") became a single attributed observation about the witnesses' reaction, the unknowns are stated plainly rather than guessed, and nothing was invented.

## 10. The reviewer's checklist

Before any account goes live — on the review screen, source and draft side by side — confirm:

- Did the AI add any detail that isn't in the source? (shape, time, location, count)
- Is every claim attributed, and does the narration avoid asserting the claim as fact?
- Are all quotes short, attributed, and load-bearing?
- Is the prose original — not mirroring a source article's wording?
- Does the classification match the rubric, with a stated reason?
- Is "What remains unknown" honestly filled in?

If all six pass, publish. If one fails, fix it or send it back — this checklist is the difference between a credible archive and a rumor mill.
